# Urban Personal Exposure Digital Twin
## Detailed Research & Competition Planning Document

> **Working concept:** A physics-constrained, uncertainty-aware, spatiotemporal urban pollution digital twin that estimates the evolving pollution field, maps a moving person's exposure through indoor and outdoor microenvironments, and optimizes their future route, timing, and schedule to reduce cumulative pollution exposure.

---

# 1. Executive Summary

## 1.1 What we are NOT building

We are explicitly **not** building merely:

- An AQI dashboard
- A weather/AQI application
- A simple AQI forecasting model
- An app that says "AQI is 183, wear a mask"
- A GPS route planner that picks the road with the lowest current AQI
- A generic "AI + AQI + recommendations" application
- A system that combines many neural networks simply to appear sophisticated

Those systems either already exist in various forms or fail to exploit the deeper scientific structure of urban pollution.

## 1.2 What we ARE building

We are building an **Urban Personal Exposure Digital Twin**.

The central idea is:

> The city has a continuously evolving pollution state, while the user has a continuously evolving location/activity/environment state. The system learns the interaction between those two states to estimate and minimize the user's future cumulative exposure.

Conceptually:

```text
URBAN POLLUTION STATE
          ×
HUMAN MOBILITY / ACTIVITY STATE
          ↓
PERSONAL EXPOSURE STATE
          ↓
OPTIMIZATION
          ↓
ACTIONABLE ASSISTANCE
```

The system therefore moves through the following pipeline:

```text
Collect
  ↓
Fuse
  ↓
Reconstruct
  ↓
Predict
  ↓
Quantify uncertainty
  ↓
Estimate personal exposure
  ↓
Optimize route/time/schedule
  ↓
Recommend actions
  ↓
Learn from repeated behavior
```

# 2. The Fundamental Problem

## 2.1 The air-quality map is not the same thing as a person's exposure

A conventional air-quality system asks:

> "What is the pollution concentration at this location?"

That is useful, but insufficient.

A human does not remain at one fixed point.

A person may:

```text
HOME
  ↓
ROAD
  ↓
COLLEGE
  ↓
ROAD
  ↓
GYM
  ↓
RESTAURANT
  ↓
HOME
```

The person's exposure changes continuously with:

- Location
- Time
- Weather
- Traffic
- Wind
- Pollution transport
- Activity intensity
- Indoor/outdoor state
- Building characteristics
- Ventilation
- Duration of exposure
- Transportation mode
- Route choice

Therefore:

> A city-wide or point-level AQI value is not equivalent to the pollution actually experienced by a moving human.

---

# 3. Why Pollution Is a Spatiotemporal Dynamical System

Pollution is not static.

It:

- Moves with wind
- Diffuses
- Is generated by traffic
- Is generated by industrial sources
- Accumulates around urban structures
- Is affected by buildings and street morphology
- Can be removed by rain
- Undergoes chemical transformation
- Changes with time
- Depends on emissions and meteorological conditions

Therefore:

> Pollution concentration at location X at time t is not independent of the surrounding spatial state and what happened in the system at earlier times.

This is fundamentally a **spatiotemporal dynamical-system problem**.

A simplified formulation is:

\[
C(x,y,t+\Delta t)
=
F(C(x,y,t),W(x,y,t),E(x,y,t),R(x,y),H(x,y),...)
\]

Where:

- \(C\) = pollutant concentration field
- \(W\) = weather / meteorological state
- \(E\) = emissions
- \(R\) = road/traffic structure
- \(H\) = urban morphology
- \(t\) = time

The objective is therefore not simply:

> Predict AQI.

It is:

> Learn how the urban pollution field evolves through space and time.

# 5. The Central Research Question

The project should be framed around:

> **Can we construct a physics-constrained, uncertainty-aware, spatiotemporal digital twin of urban pollution and use it to minimize an individual's future cumulative exposure under real-world mobility constraints?**

This is much stronger than:

> "Can we predict AQI?"

or:

> "Can we find a cleaner route?"

---

# 6. System Objectives

The complete system should aim to:

1. Collect heterogeneous urban environmental data.
2. Fuse observations from multiple sources.
3. Reconstruct a high-resolution urban pollution field.
4. Predict the future evolution of that field.
5. Respect physical constraints of pollution transport.
6. Quantify uncertainty in predictions.
7. Model human movement through the pollution field.
8. Distinguish indoor and outdoor microenvironments.
9. Estimate cumulative personal pollutant exposure.
10. Optimize routes according to predicted exposure.
11. Optimize departure times.
12. Optimize multi-stop daily schedules.
13. Trade off pollution exposure against travel time and user constraints.
14. Learn recurring mobility patterns.
15. Provide interpretable recommendations rather than raw model output.

# 11. Why GNN?

A conventional neural network treats inputs primarily as vectors.

An urban environment has a naturally relational structure.

A GNN can learn:

> How information at one urban location influences neighboring or connected locations.

This is particularly useful for:

- Spatial pollution propagation
- Sparse sensor interpolation
- Road-network effects
- Local neighborhood effects
- Dynamic pollutant transport

The key idea is:

> The graph should represent the structure through which pollution information and influence propagate.

# 12. Layer 4 — Physics-Constrained Pollution Dynamics

Purely data-driven prediction is not enough.

Urban pollution approximately follows physical processes involving:

- Advection
- Diffusion
- Emission
- Deposition
- Chemical transformation

A simplified transport formulation can resemble:

\[
\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}
+
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla C
=
D\nabla^2C
+
S
-
R
\]

Where:

- \(C\) = concentration
- \(\mathbf{u}\) = wind velocity field
- \(D\) = diffusion coefficient
- \(S\) = source/emission term
- \(R\) = removal/reaction term

The project does not need to reproduce a complete atmospheric chemistry simulator.

Instead:

> Let machine learning learn complex relationships from data while physical constraints discourage physically implausible predictions.

---

# 13. Why Physics-Informed Learning?

Without physical constraints, a model may learn correlations that work on historical data but violate physical behavior.

Physics-informed learning introduces additional structure into training.

Conceptually:

```text
Data loss
    +
Physics loss
    +
Task-specific loss
    =
Total training objective
```

For example:

\[
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_d\mathcal{L}_{data}
+
\lambda_p\mathcal{L}_{physics}
+
\lambda_r\mathcal{L}_{regularization}
\]

The physics term can penalize violations of the selected transport constraints.

This is not "using physics because it sounds advanced."

It is justified because the target system is governed by physical transport processes.

# 14. Layer 5 — Neural Operator

## 14.1 Why a neural operator?

A conventional neural network learns:

```text
Input → Output
```

A neural operator is designed to learn mappings closer to:

```text
Input function / field
          ↓
Output function / field
```

Our problem naturally has the form:

\[
C(x,y,t)
\rightarrow
C(x,y,t+\Delta t)
\]

That is a field-to-field evolution problem.

Therefore, neural operator methods are a candidate for the pollution-evolution component.

## 14.2 Potential role

The neural operator can learn:

> How an entire urban pollution field evolves under changing environmental conditions.

Potential inputs:

- Current pollution field
- Wind field
- Temperature
- Humidity
- Emission/source fields
- Traffic field
- Static urban morphology

Potential output:

- Future pollution field

# 17. Layer 6 — Uncertainty Quantification

This is a critical component.

A model operating in a city with sparse sensors will inevitably encounter regions where evidence is weak.

Example:

```text
Sensor             Sensor
   ●----------------●


          ????
       Sparse area
          ????


                     ●
                   Sensor
```

A conventional system may produce:

> PM2.5 = 137.2 μg/m³

even if the model has very little evidence.

Our system should instead communicate uncertainty.

Example:

> Predicted PM2.5: 137 ± 28 μg/m³  
> Confidence: Low

Or:

```text
Prediction: 137
Uncertainty: High
Recommendation confidence: Low
```

---

# 18. Why Uncertainty Matters

Uncertainty should propagate through the system.

Conceptually:

```text
Sensor uncertainty
       ↓
Pollution-field uncertainty
       ↓
Future-state uncertainty
       ↓
Exposure uncertainty
       ↓
Recommendation uncertainty
```

The route planner should not aggressively optimize based on a highly uncertain prediction.

The assistant should be able to say:

> "Route B appears cleaner, but confidence is low because this area has sparse monitoring coverage."

This is much more scientifically responsible.

# 24. Personal Exposure Model

The user does not experience "city AQI."

They experience a concentration over time while breathing at a certain rate.

A simplified exposure model is:

\[
E_{user}
=
\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
C(x(t),y(t),t)\dot V(t)\,dt
\]

Where:

- \(C(x(t),y(t),t)\) = concentration encountered by the user
- \(\dot V(t)\) = ventilation / inhaled air volume rate
- \(t\) = time

Very roughly:

> Exposure = concentration × inhaled air × duration.

The exact exposure/health interpretation must be carefully defined and should not pretend to be a medical measurement.

# 26. Dynamic Exposure-Aware Routing

The routing problem is not:

> "Find the route with the lowest current AQI."

It is:

> **Find a feasible route through a dynamically evolving pollution field that minimizes predicted cumulative exposure while satisfying travel-time and user constraints.**

This creates a constrained optimization problem.

Possible objective:

\[
\min_R
\left[
\alpha E(R)
+
\beta T(R)
+
\gamma U(R)
\right]
\]

Where:

- \(E(R)\) = predicted pollution exposure
- \(T(R)\) = travel time
- \(U(R)\) = uncertainty/risk
- \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) = user-configurable weights

# 30. Schedule Optimization Formulation

Represent the user's day as a sequence:

\[
S =
\{L_0,L_1,L_2,...,L_n\}
\]

with corresponding times:

\[
T =
\{t_0,t_1,...,t_n\}
\]

The optimization objective becomes something like:

\[
\min_{S,T,R}
\left[
E_{total}
+
\lambda_T T_{travel}
+
\lambda_C C_{constraints}
+
\lambda_U U_{risk}
\right]
\]

Subject to:

- Required destinations
- Appointment times
- Maximum acceptable travel time
- Opening/closing times
- User preferences
- Transportation constraints

Therefore:

# 35. Digital Twin Concept

The "digital twin" is not merely a visualization.

It consists conceptually of two continuously evolving states:

## Urban state

\[
U(t)=
\{
C(x,y,t),
W(x,y,t),
E(x,y,t),
Traffic(t),
...
\}
\]

## Human state

\[
H(t)=
\{
x(t),
y(t),
activity(t),
mode(t),
microenvironment(t),
...
\}
\]

The system estimates:

\[
P(t)
=
F(U(t),H(t))
\]

where \(P(t)\) is the person's exposure state.

Then optimization determines future decisions:

\[
a^*
=
\arg\min_a
E[P(t)|a]
\]

subject to real-world constraints.

# 36. Why the Architecture Is Justified

The architecture should be defended component-by-component.

| Component | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| Multi-source fusion | No single source completely describes urban pollution |
| Graph representation | Cities have irregular spatial/road relationships |
| GNN | Learns relational spatial propagation |
| Physics constraints | Pollution obeys physical transport processes |
| Temporal model | Pollution evolves over time |
| Neural operator | Pollution is a field-to-field evolution problem |
| Uncertainty model | Sensors are sparse/noisy and predictions can be unreliable |
| GPS trajectory | Exposure depends on where the person actually moves |
| Microenvironment model | Indoor and outdoor concentrations differ |
| Activity model | Breathing/ventilation varies with activity |
| Exposure integration | Converts concentration history into personal exposure |
| Route optimization | User can choose between feasible paths |
| Time optimization | User can choose when to travel |
| Schedule optimization | Multiple daily activities interact |
| Personalization | Users have recurring behavior and preferences |
| Feedback loop | Predictions can be corrected using observed behavior |

The guiding principle is:

> **Every sophisticated model component must correspond to a real complexity in the physical or decision-making problem.**

# 40. Evaluation

The project should evaluate four separate things.

## 40.1 Pollution prediction

Metrics may include:

- MAE
- RMSE
- MAPE where appropriate
- R²
- Spatial error
- Temporal error

## 40.2 Uncertainty calibration

Measure whether:

> "90% confidence interval"

actually contains the target approximately 90% of the time.

Possible metrics:

- Prediction interval coverage
- Calibration error
- Negative log likelihood
- CRPS where appropriate

## 40.3 Exposure estimation

Compare estimated exposure against:

- Available personal sensors
- High-quality reference measurements
- Synthetic/controlled simulations if real personal data is unavailable

## 40.4 Routing performance

Measure:

- Exposure reduction
- Additional travel time
- Distance increase
- Constraint violations
- Robustness under prediction uncertainty

The headline metric could be:

\[
\text{Exposure Reduction}
=
\frac{E_{baseline}-E_{optimized}}
{E_{baseline}}
\times 100
\]

# 44. Research Novelty Positioning

Do **not** claim:

> "Nobody has done this."

The literature already contains components involving:

- Route/activity-based exposure modelling
- Indoor/outdoor exposure modelling
- GNN-based pollution prediction

That is not a weakness.

This is much more defensible.

# 46. Privacy and Data Governance

A real implementation must treat location history as sensitive.

The system should prefer:

- On-device processing where possible
- Explicit user consent
- Minimal data retention
- Aggregated historical patterns
- User-controlled tracking
- Clear explanations of what is stored

Do not store raw continuous GPS history indefinitely if the application does not need it.

Potential architecture:

```text
Raw GPS
  ↓
On-device preprocessing
  ↓
Trajectory features
  ↓
Server / model
```

rather than:

```text
Raw GPS history
  ↓
Permanent cloud storage
```

# 47. Safety and Scientific Honesty

The system should never present estimated exposure as a clinical measurement.

Use language such as:

- Estimated exposure
- Predicted concentration
- Model confidence
- Uncertainty
- Approximate exposure reduction

Avoid statements such as:

> "You inhaled exactly 37.2 µg of PM2.5."

unless the measurement methodology genuinely supports that precision.

Similarly:

> "Low exposure"

should not automatically become:

> "Safe."

Air-quality recommendations should be framed as decision support, not medical diagnosis.
